
# Open Source Disadvantages

## The Hidden Costs of Open Source

Although open-source software (OSS) gives users considerable freedom, it also comes with problems that are easy to overlook. The fact that software is free to download does not mean it is free to develop or maintain, especially when large companies rely on projects managed by unpaid volunteers. As a result, some of the most widely used digital tools depend on a surprisingly small number of people whose work often goes unrecognized (Market Reports World, 2024).

## The Main Challenges

One major concern is **volunteer burnout**. When a project depends on only one or two developers, its future becomes uncertain if they no longer have the time or motivation to maintain it. Unlike commercial software, there may be no company contractually responsible for providing updates or fixing problems, leaving users to find alternatives themselves.

**Security** presents a similar problem. While publicly available code allows anyone to identify vulnerabilities, this advantage means little if nobody is actively reviewing it. The Heartbleed vulnerability in OpenSSL illustrates what can happen when essential software receives insufficient attention and resources. Despite being used by countless websites, OpenSSL was maintained by a relatively small team, raising questions about how the digital infrastructure we depend on is supported (Durumeric et al., 2014).

Another disadvantage is the **cost of implementation**. Organizations may avoid expensive software licenses, only to discover that installing and maintaining open-source alternatives requires specialized knowledge. Training employees or hiring IT professionals can therefore become a significant expense (Susnjara & Smalley, 2026).

## Who Is Affected?

For **students**, free programs such as LibreOffice make technology more accessible, although learning unfamiliar interfaces can be frustrating, particularly when employers continue to rely on commercial software.

**Researchers** face a different problem. If an open-source tool is abandoned and eventually becomes incompatible with newer systems, reproducing previous studies can become difficult or even impossible.

The situation is especially complicated for **volunteer maintainers**, who may spend their personal time fixing problems while corporations benefit financially from their work without contributing much in return.

For **municipalities**, the lack of guaranteed technical support can also be concerning. A local government might gain more control over its software, but when a critical system fails, it may need to hire outside specialists rather than simply contacting a vendor.
